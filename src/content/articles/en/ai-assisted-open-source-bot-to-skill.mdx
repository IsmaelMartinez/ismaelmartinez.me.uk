## The Bot I Built and Then Took Apart

In [the first article](/en/articles/ai-assisted-open-source-maintenance) I mentioned in passing [a bot](https://github.com/IsmaelMartinez/github-issue-triage-bot) I built and later retired. This is its story. I put it together to help me triage teams-for-linux issues. It ended up running serverless on Google Cloud, with embeddings over the issue history and the documentation, and for a few months it did more or less what I asked of it. Then I took most of it apart. The interesting part wasn't building it, it was deciding what to remove.

## What It Grew Into

The bot started small and out of curiosity. Bit by bit it grew. A first phase read the issue against the bug template and flagged what was missing (reproduction steps, logs), with no AI involved. Another searched the documentation for known solutions. Another looked for similar issues in the history. And for feature requests, it gathered the relevant context: roadmap, ADRs, research documents. Since this project depends on Electron, I added the issues from there to give it more context. So it was worth fitting it with a RAG and feeding in the new issues as they came.

Since I didn't trust letting it loose near real people, I kept adding scaffolding. First in silent mode, storing everything in a database and a dashboard so I could watch it without it publishing anything. Then some shadow repos that acted as an approval gate: the bot left its draft there and I promoted it or discarded it. On top of that, two safety layers (a validator and a second LLM reviewing the first) and an escalation to me if it went round in circles without progressing. And at first it could even promote responses to the main repo and reply to issues.

Then I kept adding: an MCP server, a skill to install it and one to use it, a GitHub App, an Action, OpenAPI specs, disaster recovery, even an A2A spec so repo-butler could talk to it (a story for another day, though the butler is partly what killed it, like in an Agatha Christie novel). I let it run loose for a week and some of it landed, but it was a "meh": it worked, and it gave me headaches. So I moved it to Cloud Run and Neon, added OpenTelemetry and dashboards for visibility, and an LLM to review what it generated. It was starting to get fairly accurate, and I liked it.

## Why It Stopped Paying Off

It was improving, but slowly, and something didn't add up. So I did what usually works: I put it in a drawer for a few weeks while it kept collecting data and drafting reports in the shadow repo, then came back and looked at what it had actually done.

I'd clearly overdone it. Everything fitted together, but like any system that has been around a while, it was full of things that worked and that nobody needed.

The shadow repos became a pain to maintain. Cloud Run sometimes took a while to start, and that cold start added latency. There were quite a few false positives, and some came from the Electron side, different versions that look alike in the symptoms but don't share the cause. I also tried other bots to find similar issues and they threw too many false positives. It got too complicated for what it gave back: a Death Star, the kind of thing that works but nobody can afford to keep.

The idea was to let it grow organically, but I'd run out of things that would make it better. Adding features was dead easy, and when the next one on the list was home automation I knew I'd gone too far. In a few months it had piled up the complexity you'd normally accumulate over years.

## The Human Part You Couldn't Automate

But the underlying reason wasn't technical. When we created the bot, it could promote things to the main repo and reply to issues. That created bad feeling. People didn't like a bot trying to answer their issues, even when in many cases it did it better or faster than me. I couldn't sell it, and maybe there was no way to sell it.

So I brought it back under my hand: I turned it into a skill and stripped the extras away until that was all that was left, a skill I keep improving and that I control.

## Remaking It as a Skill

So I remade it as a skill, starting simpler. In a couple of afternoons it had everything I'd accumulated. It needs no shadow repos, because I call it when I want to; it has no infrastructure behind it, so no latency; and a plain `gh issue list` gets you a long way, in Electron's tracker as well as ours.

## The One Thing That Earned Its Place

Of all that apparatus, the one thing that proved it earned its place was the embeddings. Having the issue history vectorised and linked really does help find related issues that would otherwise slip past me. That's the piece I'm keeping.

The plan is to move those embeddings out of the bot and into teams-for-linux itself, as a small committed index the skill reads directly and a CI job refreshes, with no server or database behind it. Even that is a bridge: in May 2026 GitHub shipped native semantic issue search in Copilot Chat, which is the same capability with no infrastructure on my side, and the day it is reachable from an API the index goes too. Will it hold? I don't know. Only time will tell.

## Closing Thought

I automated the building of triage and found that what was worth keeping wasn't the piece that decided for me, but the one that helped me decide. The bot didn't fail: it taught me which half of the problem was really mine.

The AI searches, compares, and summarises. I decide, verify, and respond. The same as in [the principles article](/en/articles/ai-assisted-open-source-maintenance) and [the workflow one](/en/articles/ai-assisted-open-source-workflow), and as I wrote in [The AI Automation Trap](/en/articles/the-ai-automation-trap): AI removed the building constraint, not the deciding one.
